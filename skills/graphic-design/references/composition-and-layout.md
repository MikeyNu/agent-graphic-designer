# Composition and layout

Make a visual argument. Decide what a person needs to recognize, understand, feel, and do, then organize the artifact around that purpose. Treat the methods below as working design heuristics unless a source identifies a research finding or formal requirement. Coordinate text decisions with [typography and perception](typography-and-perception.md).

## Write the visual thesis

Before decorating, complete: “The viewer should first recognize **subject**, then understand **message**, then notice **support or action**. The central visual idea is **specific relationship**.” For an awareness poster, the final action may be remembering a fact. For a report, it may be understanding a comparison. Do not force a sales CTA into every artifact.

List required content, verify its source, and rank its importance. Distinguish content that needs visual prominence from content that merely needs to be available. Preserve important qualifications, captions, and instructions; do not remove them solely to make a layout sparse.

Translate adjectives into decisions. “Energetic” could mean directional crop, rhythmic repetition, scale shifts, or expressive lettering. It does not automatically mean bright gradients and decorative arrows. “Premium” may require material sensitivity, image direction, precise spacing, or careful editing. It does not automatically mean black, gold, and a thin serif.

Identify the relevant communication environment: passing glance, phone feed, held object, projected slide, workstation, exhibition wall, or long reading. Choose a realistic test size and viewing condition before judging the result.

## Use principles as instruments

Use this matrix to diagnose what a composition needs. No row is a requirement to add an effect.

| Principle | Apply deliberately | Inspect for failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hierarchy | Give different importance levels distinguishable treatment | Several elements competing as the headline; key facts buried |
| Contrast | Differentiate through scale, value, shape, density, position, or type | Contrast everywhere, leaving no clear priority |
| Alignment | Make relationships share meaningful edges or axes | Near-alignments that look accidental; mechanical centering |
| Proximity | Place related material together | Captions grouped with the wrong object; unrelated facts fused |
| Balance | Distribute perceived weight across the field | A composition tipping unintentionally or feeling inert |
| Scale and proportion | Use relative size to express emphasis and relationship | Oversized decoration and undersized information |
| Rhythm and repetition | Establish recurrence with a useful variation | Monotony, arbitrary inconsistency, or a pattern overpowering content |
| Unity and variety | Maintain a shared visual vocabulary with purposeful differences | Several unrelated styles or a lifeless template |
| Figure and ground | Make the subject and surrounding field legible | Ambiguous silhouettes, weak separation, accidental shapes |
| Negative space | Shape pauses, groupings, emphasis, and breathing room | Empty areas without purpose; gaps that sever relationships |
| Direction and tension | Use orientation, crops, and asymmetry to create movement or anticipation | Tangencies, awkward edge pressure, or attention pulled away |

Explain decisions by their function. “The date is grouped with the location so they can be found together” is useful. “I used the proximity principle” leaves the actual improvement unspecified.

## Build the underlying structure

Start with content blocks and visual masses before polishing color or effects. A rough thumbnail can reveal whether the concept has a usable arrangement. For substantial original work, compare several structurally different thumbnails before committing to one. Do not repeat this exploration when making a narrow correction to a sound existing system.

Choose a grid that supports the material: a single reading column, paired text/image zones, a modular editorial structure, a dense reference table, or an intentionally irregular field. Use consistent margins, columns, gutters, and alignment relationships where they help. Treat the grid as scaffolding rather than a visible ornament or an obligation to fill every cell.

Define a small set of spacing relationships, then inspect them optically. Mathematical equality does not ensure perceived equality around irregular letterforms or silhouettes. Correct awkward visual edges without making arbitrary exceptions across the entire system. Avoid the false authority of mandatory golden ratios, fixed thirds, or a universal whitespace percentage.

Create stronger distinctions before adding more containers. Try grouping by spacing, alignment, type, or rules; add panels when they express a real grouping or protect readability. Dense information may benefit from a carefully designed table rather than a collection of oversized cards. A poster may benefit from one dominant image rather than several equally weighted illustrations.

Set text and imagery together. Test headline breaks against the image's shape, keep captions attached to their subjects, and preserve a useful paragraph measure. Coordinate repeated artifacts as a family while adapting their composition to different aspect ratios. Do not treat resizing as cropping alone.

## Direct images and negative space

Give each visual element a role: identify the subject, demonstrate use, establish atmosphere, explain a relationship, provide evidence, or carry a metaphor. Remove or replace elements whose only explanation is that the layout looked empty.

Choose the point of view and crop as design decisions. Compare a contextual view, a revealing detail, and a silhouette when they serve different ideas. Preserve product shape, factual relationships, meaningful gestures, and truthful context. Do not crop an evidence image to imply something it does not show.

Check the interaction of the image's main masses with the headline and page edges. Inspect awkward tangencies, partly clipped objects, accidental halos, limbs or lines meeting text, and insufficient space around the main subject. Do not assume every face must point toward text; inspect the actual effect.

Use texture, illustration, symbols, or dimensionality when they strengthen the concept. Match texture to the implied material or process. Decorative paper grain does not establish authenticity. A custom drawing is not automatically better than excellent photography, and an expensive image-generation pass is not a substitute for a strong composition.

Keep enough quiet area for the information that must be read. Where text overlays imagery, inspect the whole text region and alternate crops; an average background color cannot establish reliable contrast. Preserve expressive visual fields where expression is the task, while maintaining access to required information.

## Use perception carefully

Research on [perceptual grouping and figure-ground organization](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3482144/) describes how proximity, similarity, continuity, common region, and other cues influence visual organization. Apply those findings as hypotheses about relationships: connected objects may look related; a shared panel may imply membership. Check conflicts among cues. Do not claim that a particular layout guarantees a viewer's scan path.

Distinguish a research result from its application. [Hekkert and colleagues' study of industrial design](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12648393/) found joint contributions of novelty and typicality to aesthetic preference in its tested conditions. Use this as a reason to investigate both recognizability and surprise, not as proof that a fixed novelty percentage maximizes graphic-design quality.

Account for audience familiarity, task, reading direction, visual ability, and context. Do not claim to know every viewer's feelings. Treat pattern recognition and cultural references as situated. Research an unfamiliar symbol or historical source before using it as meaningful visual language.

Do not invent eye tracking, neuroscience, conversion gains, universal F- or Z-shaped reading paths, or “the brain processes visuals” statistics. A model's predicted response is a critique hypothesis. It is not a human participant's observation.

## Develop contrasting compositions

Use these examples as reasoning exercises, not templates. Their predictions remain untested until the actual artifacts are rendered and reviewed.

### Example 1: Community information poster

**Brief:** Announce a weekend community meal. The audience must identify the event and find the date, venue, and contact quickly.

**Route A:** Use one candid contextual photograph, a direct event title, and a compact details block. Let the photograph demonstrate the human setting. Group date and place; give the contact its own clear treatment.

**Route B:** Use a bold typographic composition with one distinctive serving-object illustration. Repeat a limited shape vocabulary to make the poster recognizable across variants.

**Compare:** Can each route communicate the event at reduced size? Does the image promise the actual experience? Are details legible at the expected distance? Does the illustration feel locally appropriate? Prefer the route that answers those questions, even if the other is more unusual.

**Reject or revise:** A collage of generic smiling people, floating icons, multiple badges, and equal-sized information blocks. The defect is competing signals and weak specificity, not the mere presence of any one ingredient.

### Example 2: Experimental music release

**Brief:** Create a sleeve about fragmentation and repair, plus a small digital thumbnail.

**Route A:** Build a restrained image of a repaired material, with a deliberate interruption in the title. Make the repair central to the composition.

**Route B:** Develop a typographic structure from repeated fragments that form a coherent whole. Use inconsistency where it conveys the music's concept; keep required credits controlled.

**Compare:** Does the idea remain visible without the explanation? Does reduction destroy the relationship? Are the fragments specific and intentional, or just generic distressed texture? Judge the sleeve and thumbnail separately and adapt their density.

**Reject or revise:** Noise scattered over every element to manufacture “human” character. Preserve the underlying relationship before adding surface treatment.

### Example 3: Technical report page

**Brief:** Explain a process bottleneck using supplied evidence.

**Route A:** Place a precisely labeled process figure beside a short interpretation, with evidence notes attached to the relevant stage.

**Route B:** Use a comparison table if the main task is comparing stages rather than following transitions.

**Compare:** Can the reader identify where the delay occurs and which observation supports that conclusion? Preserve units, scope, definitions, and qualifications. Do not make an attractive diagram imply measured causal relationships that the source does not establish.

These briefs justify different densities, levels of expression, and reading rhythms. Do not apply one preferred aesthetic to all three.

## Annotate an existing artifact

Use visual annotation when a spatial problem is easier to demonstrate than describe. Work on a duplicate, review layer, or clearly labeled overlay. Preserve the source and make proposed changes distinguishable from original content.

1. **Inspect the actual pages or frames.** Note page number, crop, dimensions, and version. Read the relevant text before suggesting edits. Extracted text alone does not reveal layout.
2. **Choose a small set of consequential issues.** Prioritize a wrong hierarchy, ambiguous grouping, unreadable information, damaged image, or contradiction between message and treatment. Avoid covering the artifact in cosmetic comments.
3. **Anchor each annotation.** Use a numbered marker, a bounded region, or a leader line. Keep comments off the information they discuss. Use shape or labels in addition to color.
4. **State observation, consequence, and action.** For example: “2. The date competes with three badges. Group the badges into one supporting line and increase separation around the date.”
5. **Demonstrate the proposed relationship.** Add a small alternative crop, alignment guide, rearranged block, type sample, or a side-by-side revision when it will clarify the recommendation.
6. **Verify the annotated export.** Reopen it. Check marker placement, legibility, source preservation, and whether its proposed changes match the written explanation.

Use available document comments, PDF annotations, vector overlays, or design-editor layers as appropriate. Follow the environment's applicable tool and artifact instructions. For a code-native diagram or vector annotation, keep labels exact and editable where possible. Do not generate a raster facsimile of a technical document and present its altered text as the original.

Label annotations as **observed defect**, **proposed change**, or **hypothesis to test** when the distinction matters. Maintain a clean version for client viewing. Never claim that an annotated suggestion has already been implemented in the source file.

## Inspect the result and revise

Inspect the rendered artifact at composition scale, intended viewing size, and close detail. Use reduced-size or squint/blur views to diagnose visual masses; grayscale can expose value relationships. These aids do not prove comprehension, disability accessibility, or actual eye movements.

Review five questions:

1. Does the subject and purpose emerge without a long rationale?
2. Can the viewer find required information in an appropriate sequence?
3. Does every prominent element deserve its prominence?
4. Does the concept belong to this content, audience, and context?
5. Do crop, alignment, spacing, edges, and repeated details feel resolved?

Use a brand-swap thought experiment to detect weak specificity: if replacing the name leaves every creative decision equally suitable, investigate whether the identity or concept needs more work. This is a heuristic, not a demand that every utilitarian graphic be unique.

Classify defects by consequence. Correct inaccurate content, obscured meaning, and failed legibility before minor spacing. For a subjective disagreement, identify what is visible and connect it to the brief. “Cheap,” “boring,” and “premium” alone are not actionable diagnoses.

Run an explicit revision loop: **criterion → observed evidence → change → rendered recheck**. Compare meaningful alternatives instead of endlessly adding decoration. Stop optional polishing when the purpose is met and no material defect remains. Report any untested physical proof, audience interpretation, or unsupported export behavior honestly.
